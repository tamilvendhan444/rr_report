Identifying the Most Reliable Players Under Pressure

SupeRR Selector Season 2 – Round 1 Methodology Report

1. Summary and final rankings

We built a leverage-based pressure index from a win-probability model, rated every batter and bowler on how much they add to their side's winning chances on high-pressure deliveries (relative to peers, after adjusting for the opposition), compared that with their ordinary-situation level, and added a consistency measure. Ratings are averaged over five reasonable definitions of "high pressure" so that the ranking does not depend on one arbitrary cut-off. The analysis uses 191,903 deliveries from 803 matches (383 IPL/T20 league matches and 420 T20Is, 2022–2026).

2. Data and preparation

  • Files used: ball-by-ball deliveries (200,999 rows, 867 matches), matches, competitions, teams, playing XI, and competition-team-player mapping. Player names come from the ball-by-ball file.
  • Match filter: Matches with reduced overs, revised targets or D/L(S) results, abandoned/no-result matches, and matches whose innings did not finish were excluded because win probability is not comparable there. Super-over innings were excluded. 803 matches remain (191,903 deliveries; 1st and 2nd innings only).
  • Run attribution: Batter runs are bat runs only; wides are charged to the bowler and are not a ball faced; leg-byes, byes, and penalty runs are credited to neither player; no-ball extras are charged to the bowler. Run-outs and retired-outs are not charged to the bowler; a non-striker run-out is not charged to the striker.
  • Legal ball: Not a wide, no-ball or penalty row. Wickets = rows with a dismissal type.

3. Defining pressure

Pressure is defined as how much the next ball can swing the match: the leverage index (LI). It combines every signal the brief mentions (required run rate, wickets in hand, balls remaining, innings stage, closeness) automatically because it is computed from an empirical model of the match state.

3.1 Win-probability model

We fit gradient-boosted classifiers on the state after each delivery, separately for innings 1 (features: legal balls left, wickets lost, runs scored, T20I flag) and innings 2 (balls left, wickets lost, runs required, target, T20I flag), with monotonic constraints (more runs required or more wickets lost can never help). Predictions are out-of-fold (5 folds grouped by match), so no delivery is scored by a model that saw its match. Terminal chase states are set exactly (target reached = 1, all out or balls exhausted = 0, tie = 0.5).

Out-of-fold Brier score: 0.223 (innings 1; a coin-flip is 0.25) and 0.145 (chase). Calibration is reliable in the middle and slightly over-confident at the extremes.

3.2 Leverage index and the high-pressure threshold

For each delivery, $\text{LI} = \sum_{o \in \text{outcomes}} P(o) \times |\Delta \text{Win Probability}|$. Outcome probabilities come from the empirical data by innings, phase (overs 1–6, 7–15, 16–20) and wickets-lost band. LI is divided by its overall mean, so 1.0 = an average ball. We define HIGH PRESSURE when $\text{LI} \ge 1.5\times$ average, which represents 18.0% of deliveries (34,623 balls); 1.0–1.5× is "elevated" and below 1.0× is "ordinary".

4. Performance measurement and rating

4.1 What is measured

Each delivery has a win-probability change $\text{dWP}$ (from the batting side's view). The batter's per-ball value is $\text{dWP} / \text{LI}$ (scaled by average LI), expressing how much of the possible swing the player captured; the bowler's value is the exact negative.

4.2 Opposition adjustment and shrinkage

Ball values are modelled as $\text{batter effect} + \text{bowler effect}$ using a sparse ridge regression (separately for high-pressure and ordinary deliveries). The opposing player's effect is estimated simultaneously, so facing weaker bowling attacks does not inflate a batter. The ridge penalty ($\lambda = 600$, chosen by 5-fold match-grouped cross-validation) is an empirical-Bayes shrinkage: players with few high-pressure balls are pulled toward the average.

4.3 Three components and the composite

  • Pressure rating (50%): The shrunken high-pressure batter/bowler effect.
  • Rise vs usual (25%): Pressure rating minus the same player's rating on ordinary deliveries (personal baseline).
  • Consistency (25%): Share of pressure appearances (matches with ≥3 high-pressure balls) with a positive contribution after opposition adjustment, shrunk toward 50% with 10 pseudo-appearances.

Components are standardised ($z$-scores) among eligible players and combined 50/25/25.

4.4 Ensemble over pressure definitions

The final score is the average composite across five specifications: $\text{LI} \ge 1.5$ (base), $\text{LI} \ge 1.25$, $\text{LI} \ge 2.0$, chase-only (2nd innings, $\text{LI} \ge 1.5$), and raw $\text{dWP}$ with no leverage scaling. Final ranks sort by this average.

5. Fair comparison

  • Eligibility: Batters need ≥100 high-pressure balls faced, bowlers ≥72 high-pressure legal balls (12 overs), and both need ≥15 matches containing high-pressure balls. This leaves 109 batters and 131 bowlers.
  • Contexts: IPL and T20I deliveries are pooled; the opposition adjustment links the two through players who appear in both.
  • Roles: Players are compared strictly on the pressure deliveries they faced/bowled, never on aggregate innings totals.

6. Reliability and limitations

  • Rank stability (bootstrap): Resampling matches with replacement 30 times shows Iyer and Gill (93% each) and Hazlewood (93%) are highly stable in the top tier; places 3–10 have 30–60% retention.
  • Rise vs usual: Top players like Gill, Iyer, Kohli, and Hazlewood are not necessarily "risers" but elite players whose performance does not drop under pressure. Others like Saim Ayub (+0.232), Taskin Ahmed (+0.062), and Ashwin (+0.119) rise noticeably above baseline.
  • Split-half consistency: Batter pressure ratings carry a repeatable signal ($r = 0.17$, Spearman-Brown corrected 0.30); individual bowler ratings carry higher noise due to smaller sample sizes (100–400 balls).
